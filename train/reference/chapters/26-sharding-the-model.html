Section 05 · More Than One GPU

Sharding the model across GPUs

Chapter 26 of 30 ⏱ 10 min read 📊 6 figures FSDP2

ZeRO-3 in practice: what lives on each rank, why 35.56B parameters means 498 GB of optimizer state, and the arithmetic that decides how many cards a model needs.

r1 fits on one H200 and was never sharded. Its 1.02 billion parameters, its optimizer state and its activations all lived on a single card for all 38,147 steps, and every number in the rest of this book that describes the completed run describes a single-process job with no collective communication in it at all.

This chapter is about the models above r1, which do not fit. It is included because the arithmetic that decides when a model stops fitting is the same arithmetic at every scale, because the reader who follows this book to a larger model will meet all of it, and because the sharded path is where three of the most instructive bugs in the project were found. Every measurement here comes from the flagship at 35.56 billion parameters on two B300s, and the flagship failed. The distributed machinery worked; what killed the run was routing, at step 890 of 76,294.

The arithmetic that forces the decision

A parameter is not one number in memory. During training it is several, and how many depends on the optimizer.

Our flagship holds 35.56 billion parameters. Training it in bf16 with AdamW and fp32 master weights costs 14 bytes per parameter, made up of four things: the bf16 copy the forward pass actually multiplies with, at 2 bytes; an fp32 master copy of the weight, at 4 bytes; and AdamW's two moment estimates, at 4 bytes each. The master copy exists because a bf16 weight has roughly three decimal digits of precision, and a learning rate of 3.46e-4 applied to a weight of order 1 produces an update that rounds away entirely if the accumulation happens in bf16.

Multiply it out: 35.56e9 × 14 bytes = 498 GB.

Two B300s hold 288 GB each, so 576 GB in total, and 498 GB is 86% of that. Under ZeRO-3 the persistent state divides evenly across the two ranks, so each card carries about 249 GB, leaving roughly 39 GB per card for everything else. Gradients are not in the 14 bytes. They are bf16 and they are reduce-scattered rather than held whole, so they come out of the same 39 GB that activations do.

This calculation is worth doing before renting anything, and it is the calculation most often skipped, because the parameter count is the number everyone quotes and the byte multiplier is the number nobody does. A model whose weights fit comfortably in bf16 on a card can be seven times too large to train on it.1 The multiplier changes with the optimizer. Plain SGD with momentum in bf16 is far cheaper; 8-bit optimizer states cut the two moment terms from 8 bytes to 2. We did not use either, because the recipe was meant to match what large open runs do, and every one of them we could check trains in bf16 with fp32 master weights and fp32 moments.

What ZeRO-3 actually does

Data parallelism in its simplest form replicates the model on every GPU and averages gradients. That halves nothing: each rank still holds all 498 GB. ZeRO-3, which PyTorch implements as FSDP2 and exposes through fully_shard, removes the replication.

Parameters, gradients and optimizer state are each split across ranks. With two ranks, each card holds half of every weight tensor, half of every gradient and half of every moment. A half-weight cannot be multiplied by anything, so before a module runs, its shard is all-gathered so that the full weight exists briefly on every rank; the module runs; the full copy is released. On the way back, the gradient is reduce-scattered, which averages it across ranks and hands each rank only the slice it is responsible for.

The memory a rank holds is therefore roughly total / world_size plus one module's working set, rather than total. That is the entire trade, and it is a trade of memory for communication.

We apply fully_shard to each KimiDecoderLayer first and to the root model afterwards, in that order, so that the root wraps already-sharded children rather than raw parameters. Two details around that call cost real debugging time.

The first is that every structural change to the model must happen before sharding. Our training build swaps the released gate for a trainable one, stacks the per-expert weights into fused [E, out, in] tensors, fuses the situ activation and initialises KDA's timestep bias. Sharding first turns the targets into DTensors, and the copy that installs a replacement weight then fails with got mixed torch.Tensor and DTensor. The optimizer has the opposite constraint: it must be constructed after sharding, or AdamW captures the unsharded tensors and the sharding buys nothing at all.

The second is the router bias. e_score_correction_bias is the aux-loss-free balancer's state, updated by rule after every optimizer step rather than by gradient, and we already hold it out of AdamW for that reason. It is also held out of sharding:

ignored = set(router_parameters(model))
if "ignored_params" in inspect.signature(fully_shard).parameters:
    kw["ignored_params"] = ignored

for m in model.modules():
    if isinstance(m, KimiDecoderLayer):
        fully_shard(m, **kw)          # inner modules first
fully_shard(model, **kw)              # then the root

Replicating it removes the aten::add_.Tensor got mixed torch.Tensor and DTensor crash that ended the first probe, and it makes routing identical on every rank by construction rather than identical if the sharding arithmetic happens to be right. It costs one float per expert per MoE layer, which at 512 experts is a few kilobytes per layer against the 71 GB the bf16 weights occupy.2 The balancer's own update also needs an all-reduce, because noaux_tc is defined on the global expert load and each rank only sees its own. That is one of the three bugs in the next chapter, and it is a different problem from this one: replicating the bias makes the storage consistent, all-reducing the load makes the update consistent.

One further precaution belongs to FSDP2 rather than to us. FSDP2 warns when a wrapped module returns a view tensor, because an in-place operation on a returned view silently drops the pre-backward hook, skips the all-gather, and produces wrong gradients with no error. KimiDecoderLayer returns a reshaped tensor from the attention-residual path, and establishing by inspection that no aliasing survives 24 layers is not the kind of proof worth relying on when the failure mode is a completed multi-day run that is quietly worse. We clone any returned tensor whose _base is not None, which costs one copy per layer and nothing in the common case.

What sharding costs, and why a mixture of experts pays more

Every all-gather and every reduce-scatter is traffic. For a dense model that traffic is predictable: two collectives per layer per step, each moving a known number of bytes, overlappable with computation if the implementation is careful.

A mixture of experts adds a second kind of traffic on top of the first. When experts are distributed across devices, a token routed to an expert that lives on another rank has to travel there and its result has to travel back. That is an all-to-all exchange, and unlike the all-gathers it is data-dependent: which tokens go where is decided by the router at run time, and a step in which one expert is unusually popular moves more bytes than a step in which load is even. The all-to-all traffic scales with how many ways the experts are split, which is why expert parallelism is something to minimise rather than something to maximise.

That is the argument the hardware decision was actually made on.

Choosing the card

Three options were priced, at the rates we were paying:

GPU$/hourmemory per card
H100$3.9580 GB
B200$6.25180 GB
B300$7.10288 GB

The B300 is the most expensive per hour and was chosen anyway, on a memory argument rather than a throughput argument. At 288 GB per card, two cards hold the whole optimizer state, which collapses expert parallelism from eight-way to two-way and cuts the all-to-all traffic that dominates mixture-of-experts training. The H100 route needs four to eight cards for the same state, and every extra card widens the exchange the router forces on every layer of every step.

The argument then weakened, and the weakening is worth stating because it is the sort of thing that quietly stops being checked. When the hardware was chosen, the flagship was a smaller model on paper — the design target was 567M active parameters, a figure later found to be unreachable, since everything running before a single routed expert is counted already came to 834M. The architecture correction that followed moved the total parameter count to 35.56 billion, and the same 14-byte multiplier put the optimizer state at 498 GB, or 86% of the two-card budget. Two B300s still hold it, so the decision survived. The reason the decision was made, however, was headroom, and the headroom was gone. We kept the choice and rewrote the justification to be about all-to-all traffic instead, which is a different claim and a weaker one.

What was verified, and what it measured

The flagship was not allowed to start until the sharded path had run end to end. The probe trains for four real steps at the flagship's own configuration, with an optimizer step and a balancer update, and reports memory after the first optimizer step rather than only at the end, because the first step is where both Adam moments are allocated and where the previous attempt had died.

On 2 x B300, 24 layers sharded and 24 checkpointed:3 Activation checkpointing has to be enabled after sharding, because the implementation wraps KimiDecoderLayer.forward, and wrapping before fully_shard places the checkpoint wrapper outside FSDP's module hooks, so the recomputed forward would run without the all-gather it needs. It also has to be the real one: as the chapter on the released code describes, gradient_checkpointing_enable() sets a flag this modeling code never reads.

dead experts0.0%
load imbalance3.3
gradient norm30.5 → 14.75
peak memory177.9 GB of 287.4 (62%)

Four steps is not training, and this table is not evidence that the flagship would have trained well. It is evidence of four narrower things: the model fits with headroom through a full optimizer step, FSDP2 composes with our fused expert weights and our custom autograd functions, the loss is finite and falling, and the router is alive on both ranks. A starting loss of 12.313 is close to ln(163,840) = 12.007, the cross-entropy of a uniform distribution over the vocabulary, which is the same initialisation check r1 passed at 12.100.

One number in that table does not reconcile with the arithmetic at the top of this chapter, and we are reporting rather than explaining it. The 14-byte calculation predicts about 249 GB of persistent state per card, and the measured peak was 177.9 GB. We did not decompose the gap. The arithmetic is a budget written before the run and the peak is a measurement taken during it, and when the two disagree the measurement is the one to plan against.

The card the code asked for was not the card the plan chose

The plan specified two B300s and the 288 GB figure is what all of the arithmetic above assumes. The code that launched the probe and the flagship both contained gpu="B200:2", hardcoded, and had contained it from the beginning.

The result was a memory failure that looked like a model problem. Run on 2 x B200 at the smallest batch and sequence length the model accepts, the job placed both shards, completed a forward pass, and then went out of memory while allocating AdamW's second moment. Sharding itself was working. The model was split correctly across both cards. What did not fit was the last of the four per-parameter terms, on a card of 180 GB rather than 288, which is 108 GB less per rank than every calculation above had assumed.4 The failure arriving at the second moment rather than at the forward pass is consistent with something we see elsewhere in the project: peak memory on this model is set by the optimizer allocation rather than by activations, which is why it barely moves with the input shape. The silent-fallback chapter records the same signature, where peak memory was identical at 8,192 and 32,768 tokens per step. Reported as an observation, not a conclusion.

Both entrypoints are now B300:2. The transferable part is not the fix. It is that the constraint lived in two places, a planning document and a decorator, with nothing checking that they agreed, and the disagreement surfaced as an allocator error at the least informative possible moment. A configuration value that an expensive decision depends on should be asserted somewhere the run can read it.

The launch that reads exactly like a crash

One operational rule belongs in this chapter because it was learned here and because it costs a multi-day run if it is learned later.

--detach is mandatory. A probe spawned without it produced a normal image build, then the line Stopping app - local entrypoint completed, and then a FunctionCall that raised RemoteError with an empty message. Three of those four signals read as a crash in the probe. None of them is one. The ephemeral app stops when the local entrypoint returns, and it takes the spawned call with it, so what the error actually reports is that the work was cancelled by its own launcher.

The general shape is worth keeping. An error with no message is usually not the failure it appears to be, because a process that fails on its own has something to say about it. An empty message means the process was ended from outside, and the thing that ended it is what to go and look at.

Where this leads

The sharded path fits, runs, allocates its optimizer state with 38% of the card to spare, and produces a falling loss with a live router on both ranks. Everything in this chapter is about making a model that does not fit run at all, and all of it is verifiable by things crashing or not crashing.

The bugs in the next chapter are not like that. Sharding a training loop moves three quantities that were previously local — the tokens each process reads, the expert load the balancer responds to, and the decision to skip a step — into quantities that must be agreed across ranks. Each of the three was wrong in our first sharded implementation, and none of the three raises an error, produces a stack trace, or leaves a mark on the loss curve. Each one completes the run and returns a model that is quietly wrong.
